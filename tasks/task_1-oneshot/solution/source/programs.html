Beginner glossary

Short, plain-language definitions for the terms you'll see across Beacon Impact. Click any row to expand.

Open the full guide
Outcome
The change in someone's life that your program causes — a student reading at grade level, a family no longer skipping meals, a graduate placed in a paying job. Outcomes are the "so what" behind your work. They take longer to measure than outputs, but they're what funders and your community actually care about.
Output
The countable things you produced — meals served, classes taught, coats handed out, hours of tutoring delivered. Outputs are easy to track and prove activity happened, but they don't on their own show whether lives improved. Always pair outputs with at least one outcome.
Cost per outcome
Total program spend divided by the number of outcomes achieved. For Homework Harbor that means the full cost of a term (tutors, snacks, space) divided by students who finished the term reading at grade level. It's a useful efficiency signal — but a higher figure isn't automatically bad. Some outcomes are simply harder to produce.
Logic model
A one-page diagram that walks left to right: inputs (money, staff, volunteers) → activities (what you do) → outputs (what you produce) → outcomes (the change that follows) → impact (long-term effect on the community). Most grant applications ask for one. Beacon Impact builds a draft for you from your program data.
Beneficiary
The person your program is designed to help. Some programs serve beneficiaries directly (a child in tutoring); others reach them through someone else (a parent who attends a workshop on their child's behalf). Be specific in your reporting — "324 children, ages 5–12" is more credible than "many young people."
Theory of change
A plain-English sentence (or paragraph) that explains why you believe your activities will lead to the outcomes you promise. Example: "If we provide free weekly tutoring plus a meal, then students will attend regularly and improve at reading, because hunger and inconsistent attendance are the biggest barriers we've seen."
Reach vs. depth
Reach is how many people you touched; depth is how much you changed each person's situation. A coat drive has wide reach and shallow depth — that's fine, it's the goal. A job-readiness cohort has narrow reach and deep impact. Healthy nonprofits usually run a mix of both.
