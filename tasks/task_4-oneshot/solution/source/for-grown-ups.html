For parents, teachers, and curious grown-ups

Every paper on Starlit begins as a real piece of peer-reviewed astronomy research — something published in journals like Nature Astronomy, The Astrophysical Journal, or arXiv preprints from working scientists. We pick papers that touch a kid-friendly question (Why does Mars look red? How loud is a black hole?) and pass them to our translation team.

A trained science writer drafts the kid version. Then an educator reviews it for age-appropriate language and reading level. Then a working astronomer reads it side-by-side with the original to make sure nothing has wandered off course. Only after all three signatures does a paper appear in the galaxy. The whole loop takes about four weeks per paper.

Why we built this

Real science, sized for small humans.

Kids ask the best questions in the universe. We wanted somewhere they could find real answers — not dumbed down, just unpacked — written by people who respect both the science and the reader.

The translation pipeline

From discovery to bedtime story in three careful steps

Each paper passes through three sets of hands. Here are the humans who make the magic happen — and what they actually do at each step.

1 Original paper

Pick & decode

A science writer reads the full paper, talks to the authors when possible, and drafts a plain-language version aimed at curious nine-year-olds. Tricky terms get a "Big Words Explained" entry.

Dr. Maya Halvorsen

Science writer · ex-NASA JPL communications

2 Educator review

Level & scaffold

A classroom teacher checks reading level (Lexile 500–900), flags unclear analogies, and adds discussion prompts. They also pick the questions that go into the "Wonder more" sidebar.

Ms. Priya Okafor

5th-grade science teacher · NYC Public Schools

3 Kid-friendly version

Verify & publish

A working astronomer reads draft and original together. They fix any drift, approve every analogy, and write the one-line "What the scientist would tell you" note that closes each piece.

Dr. Tomás Reyes-Bell

Astrophysicist · University of Edinburgh

Frequently asked

Questions grown-ups ask us

If your question isn't here, write us at grownups@starlitpapers.org — a real human will reply within two school days.

What reading levels do you target, and how do I know which is right for my child?

Every paper is published in three voices: Ages 6–8 (Lexile 350–600, short sentences, lots of comparison to familiar things like pizzas and basketballs), Ages 9–11 (Lexile 600–900, our default), and Ages 12+ (Lexile 900–1100, closer to the original abstract). You can switch versions any time using the toggle in the top bar — your place in the article is preserved.

How do you make sure the simplified version is still scientifically accurate?

Every translation is read side-by-side with the original paper by a working astronomer in that subfield. If a simplification would create a misconception (for example, calling a black hole a "vacuum cleaner"), we either rewrite it or pull the paper entirely. Each finished article ends with a "What the scientist would tell you" line written by the reviewer.

I'm worried about screen time. Can my child use Starlit offline or in print?

Yes. Every paper has a "Print this paper" button that produces a black-and-white, illustration-rich PDF designed for paper reading — no ads, no links, big margins for notes. Premium families can also download our monthly bound zine, mailed to your home, which collects six papers and a sky-tonight chart.

What accessibility features does Starlit support?

Every paper has a read-aloud audio version recorded by a human narrator (no synthetic voices). Text can be resized up to 200% without breaking the layout, all images carry descriptive alt text, and our color palette is tested to WCAG AA contrast in both light and dark modes. Dyslexia-friendly font and reduced-motion options live in Settings → Reading comfort.

How can I suggest a paper for translation?

Send us a DOI, arXiv link, or even a news article that piqued your kid's curiosity at suggest@starlitpapers.org. We review submissions every other Friday. We especially love suggestions tied to a question a child actually asked — bonus points if you tell us the question. About one in five suggestions becomes a published translation, usually within 8–12 weeks.

One more thing

Starlit Papers is a nonprofit project run by a tiny team of educators, scientists, and illustrators. We're free for families and classrooms, forever. Thanks for trusting us with your kid's wonder.
